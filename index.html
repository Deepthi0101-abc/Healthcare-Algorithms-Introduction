1.1 Basic ideas

Data, information and decision

Data is raw facts, such as a temperature of 101 °F. Information is data with meaning, such as “this patient has a fever”. A decision is what we do with that information, such as “send the patient to the fever clinic”. Algorithms move us from data to decision in a fixed, repeatable way.

What is an algorithm?

An algorithm is a finite set of clear, ordered steps that takes an input, processes it, and produces an output. A cooking recipe is an everyday algorithm: ingredients are the input, the cooking steps are the process, and the dish is the output.

Characteristics of a good algorithm

  • Input: zero or more inputs.
  • Output: at least one result.
  • Definiteness: every step is clear and has only one meaning.
  • Finiteness: it stops after a limited number of steps.
  • Effectiveness: every step is simple enough to be carried out.

How an algorithm is written down

  • Flowchart: a diagram with standard shapes: oval for start and end, rectangle for an action, diamond for a decision, arrows for the flow.
  • Pseudocode: the steps in plain, structured language.

Pseudocode for a fever alert:

READ temperature
IF temperature > 38 THEN
    PRINT "Fever - refer to fever clinic"
ELSE
    PRINT "No fever"
END IF

Worked example: triage with nested IF

A clinic sends adults with chest pain or breathing difficulty to emergency, those with a temperature above 38 °C to the fever clinic, and everyone else to general OPD.

READ chest_pain, breathing_difficulty, temperature
IF chest_pain = YES OR breathing_difficulty = YES THEN
    PRINT "Send to Emergency"
ELSE IF temperature > 38 THEN
    PRINT "Send to Fever Clinic"
ELSE
    PRINT "Send to General OPD"
END IF

As a flowchart it reads top to bottom. Every diamond has two exits, Yes and No, and every path ends at an End oval.

1.2 Overview of health algorithms

Definition

A health algorithm uses health-related data, such as symptoms, vital signs, lab results or images, to support a decision, give a prediction, or raise an alert in patient care or health services.

Parts of a health algorithm

  • Input: patient data (age, blood pressure, lab values, images).
  • Processing: the rules, formula or model applied to the data.
  • Output: a risk category, score, diagnosis suggestion or alert.
  • Validation and feedback: checking the output against real outcomes and improving the algorithm over time.

Worked example: the APGAR score

APGAR checks a newborn at 1 and 5 minutes after birth. Five signs are each scored 0, 1 or 2 and added to a total out of 10. A higher score means the baby is doing better. Mapped to the four parts: input is the five signs; processing is giving 0, 1 or 2 points and adding them; output is the total; validation is checking that low scores really go with babies who need help.

Where health algorithms are used

  • Triage: how urgently a patient needs care.
  • Risk scoring: chance of a disease or complication, such as heart disease risk.
  • Clinical decision support (CDS): alerts inside the EHR.
  • Dose calculation: a safe drug dose from weight, age and kidney function.
  • Medical imaging: detecting problems on X-ray, CT, retina or skin images.
  • Monitoring: alerts when wearable or ICU readings cross a limit.
  • Hospital operations: bed demand, staff needs, appointment no-shows.
  • Public health: tracking and predicting outbreaks.

Clinical decision support (CDS) means computer tools that give clinicians the right information at the right time, usually inside the EHR. Examples: a drug-drug interaction warning, an allergy alert, an overdue-vaccination reminder, and a risk score on the patient’s screen. CDS is one of the most common ways health algorithms reach the bedside.

Benefits

  • Consistency: the same input always gives the same output.
  • Speed: results in seconds, even for large data.
  • Scale: serves thousands of patients at once.
  • Fewer errors: removes slips caused by tiredness or workload.
  • Support for decisions: a data-based second opinion for doctors.

Challenges and limitations

  • Data quality: wrong, incomplete or old data gives wrong results (“garbage in, garbage out”).
  • Bias: groups under-represented in training data may be served badly.
  • Lack of explainability: some models cannot show why they gave an answer.
  • Privacy and security: health data is sensitive.
  • Clinical validation: test on real patients before use.
  • Over-reliance: the clinician remains responsible.
  • Alert fatigue: too many alerts get ignored.

Algorithm, AI, machine learning and deep learning

AI is the wide field of making computers do tasks that normally need human intelligence. Machine learning (ML) is the part of AI where the computer learns patterns from data instead of being given every rule. Deep learning is the part of ML that uses many-layered neural networks, and works especially well on images, sound and signals. Not every algorithm is AI: a BMI calculator follows a fixed formula and does not learn.

Types based on the task performed

  • Classification: puts a patient into a category (diabetic or not; benign or malignant).
  • Regression: predicts a number (glucose tomorrow; length of stay).
  • Clustering: groups similar patients when no categories are given.
  • Optimisation: finds the best arrangement (theatre scheduling, bed allocation).

Which type should we use?

Use rule-based when the logic is clear and must be fully explainable (drug alerts). Use a scoring system when a validated clinical formula exists. Use machine learning when there is plenty of data and the pattern is too complex to write as rules. Use deep learning for images and signals when large data is available. Real hospitals often combine them.

Scoring system and deep learning: which is easier to trust?

A doctor usually finds a scoring system easier to trust because it shows exactly how the result is calculated and has been tested in many studies. A deep learning model can be more accurate on complex data such as images, but often cannot show why it gave an answer, so doctors need extra evidence first.

Use a scoring system when a validated formula exists and the decision must be easy to explain. Use deep learning when the data is images or signals, large data is available, and a clinician checks the result.

Why an algorithm can work in one hospital and fail in another

Causes

  • Different patients: city vs rural age, diseases, lifestyle.
  • Different data quality: records less complete or entered differently.
  • Different equipment: scanners and analysers differ.

More causes

  • Language and documentation habits: notes are written differently.
  • Bias in training data: missing groups are served poorly.
  • Change over time: models slowly become outdated.

The remedy is to test on local data before use and keep monitoring afterwards.

Worked case: choosing an algorithm for a real problem

Problem: a hospital wants to warn doctors when a ward patient may be getting worse.

Data neededVital signs (pulse, blood pressure, temperature, oxygen level, breathing rate), lab results, age and history.
Choice of algorithmStart with a rule-based or scoring tool such as an early warning score: simple, fast, easy to explain. With plenty of good past data, a machine learning model may catch patterns a score misses.
BenefitsEarlier alerts, consistent monitoring of every patient, less reliance on one nurse noticing a change.
RisksBias if training data does not represent all patients, alert fatigue, over-trust in the alert, and patient data privacy.
Role of the doctorThe algorithm supports the decision. The clinician stays responsible and can override the alert.

Unit I at a glance

  • Algorithm = finite, clear steps from input to output, written as a flowchart or pseudocode.
  • Health algorithm = patient data in; decision, prediction or alert out.
  • Uses: triage, risk scores, CDS alerts, dose calculation, imaging, monitoring, operations, public health.
  • Benefits: consistency, speed, scale, fewer errors. Limits: data quality, bias, explainability, privacy, validation, over-reliance.
  • Four types: rule-based, scoring, machine learning, deep learning.
  • Task types: classification, regression, clustering, optimisation.
  • Not every algorithm is AI. Deep learning is inside ML, and ML is inside AI.
